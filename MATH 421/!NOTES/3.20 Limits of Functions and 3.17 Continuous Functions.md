## Functions vs. Sequences (and $f(x_n)$)

- A **function** $f$ has a **domain** $\operatorname{dom}(f)\subseteq\mathbb R$ and outputs a real number for each $x$ in its domain. For $f(x)=\tfrac1x$, the *natural domain* is $\{x\in\mathbb R: x\neq 0\}$.  
- A **sequence** $(x_n)$ is just a function from $\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$.  
- The “sequence $f(x_n)$” is the **composed sequence** $\big(f(x_1),f(x_2),\dots\big)$; i.e., plug each $x_n$ into $f$.

**Mini-check:** with $x_n=\tfrac1n$ and $f(x)=\tfrac1x$, we get $f(x_n)=n$ (diverges to $+\infty$).

---

## 20.1 Definition — Limit via Sequences

> **What it says (formal):** $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L$ **iff** for every sequence $(x_n)$ in the domain with $x_n\to a$ (and $x_n\neq a$ if $a\notin\operatorname{dom}(f)$), we have $f(x_n)\to L$.  

> **Plain English:** To prove a function limit at $a$, it’s enough to test all “ways of approaching $a$” by sequences; the outputs must approach the same number $L$. To **disprove** a limit, find one “bad” sequence to $a$ whose images don’t go to the same $L$.

---

## 20.8 Corollary — Limits via $\varepsilon$-$\delta$

> **What it says (formal gist):** The sequence definition above is **equivalent** to the usual $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition of $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L$.  

> **Plain English:** Either approach (sequences **or** $\varepsilon,\delta$) is fair game. Use whichever is easier for the problem.

---

## 20.4 Theorem — Algebraic Limit Laws (for functions)

Let $L_1=\lim_{x\to a} f_1(x)$ and $L_2=\lim_{x\to a} f_2(x)$ be finite. Then:
1) $\lim_{x\to a}[f_1+f_2]=L_1+L_2$  
2) $\lim_{x\to a}[f_1f_2]=L_1L_2$  
3) $\lim_{x\to a}\big[\tfrac{f_1}{f_2}\big]=\tfrac{L_1}{L_2}$ if $L_2\neq 0$ and $f_2(x)\neq 0$ near $a$.  

> **Plain English:** Sums, products, and (legal) quotients behave the way you expect at limits.

---

## 20.9 Discussion — The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ Template

> **Key pattern:** “For each **$\varepsilon>0$** there exists **$\delta>0$** such that **$0<|x-a|<\delta$** implies **$|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$**.” The section lists the small tweaks when $L=\pm\infty$ or $a$ is a one-sided/at-infinity limit.  

> **Plain English:** Memorize the four blanks: **$\varepsilon>0$** | **$\delta>0$** | **closeness of $x$ to $a$** | **closeness of $f(x)$ to $L$**. Then adapt to one-sided or infinite cases.

---

## 20.10 Theorem — Two One-Sided Limits

> **What it says:** $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists **iff** both one-sided limits $\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x)$ exist **and are equal**.  

> **Plain English:** Full limit at $a$ = left limit equals right limit.

---

## 17.1 Definition — Continuity (via sequences)

> **What it says:** $f$ is **continuous at $x_0$** iff for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$ (with $x_n\in\operatorname{dom}(f)$), we have $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$. 

> **Plain English:** Inputs that get close to $x_0$ force outputs to get close to $f(x_0)$.

---

## 17.2 Theorem — Continuity and $\varepsilon$-$\delta$

> **What it says:** The sequence definition above is equivalent to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition of continuity at $x_0$.  

> **Plain English:** To prove continuity at $x_0$, you can do either a sequence proof **or** an $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proof.

---

## 17.3–17.5 — Basic Properties of Continuous Functions

- **Scalar multiple & absolute value:** If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, then $kf$ and $|f|$ are continuous at $x_0$.  
- **Algebra of continuity:** If $f,g$ are continuous at $x_0$, then $f+g$, $fg$, and $f/g$ (when $g(x_0)\ne0$) are continuous at $x_0$.  
- **Compositions & domains:** Definitions of $f+g,\,fg,\,f/g,\,g\circ f$ and where they’re defined. “These new functions are continuous if $f$ and $g$ are continuous.” (This covers the “composition of continuous functions is continuous” pattern.)  

> **Plain English:** Once you know $f$ and $g$ are continuous, you can safely build with them—add, multiply, divide (where defined), take absolute value, and **compose**—and the result stays continuous at the same point (again, **where it’s defined**).

## Tiny worked examples (match your quiz style)

### A. “What’s the difference between $f(x)=\tfrac1x$ and $x_n=\tfrac1n$, and what is $f(x_n)$?”

- $f$ is a real-valued function on $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ (its natural domain).  
- $(x_n)$ is a sequence (domain $\mathbb N$).  
- $f(x_n)=f\!\left(\tfrac1n\right)=n$ (diverges).

### B. Continuity of compositions in your quiz:

Let $f(x)=\tfrac1x$, $g(x)=x+3$.

- $g\circ f(x)=g\!\left(\tfrac1x\right)=\tfrac1x+3$.  
  **Domain:** $x\neq 0$. **Discontinuity:** at $x=0$ only (not in domain). By 17.3–17.5 rules, it’s continuous on its domain.  
- $f\circ g(x)=f(x+3)=\tfrac1{x+3}$.  
  **Domain:** $x\neq -3$. **Discontinuity:** at $x=-3$ only (not in domain). Also continuous on its domain by the same rules.  

> **Quiz tip:** When they ask “Where is $g\circ f$ **not continuous**?”, they usually mean “where does the formula break (domain holes)?”—identify those $x$.

### C. Using 20.1 quickly (limits by sequences)

To show $\lim_{x\to 0} x^2\sin(\tfrac1x)=0$: take any sequence $x_n\to0$. Then $|x_n^2\sin(\tfrac1{x_n})|\le x_n^2$, so the image goes to $0$. (This mirrors how the book uses 17.3–17.5 to talk about such examples.)
