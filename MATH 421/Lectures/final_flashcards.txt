Front: State the Intermediate Value Theorem.  
Back: If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(L\) is any value between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), then there exists \(c \in [a,b]\) such that \(f(c) = L\).

Back: \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\) if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|x - x_0| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon.\)

Front: Challenge: Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\). Does EVT imply IVT?

Front: State the Mean Value Theorem (Theorem 29.3).

Front: State the definition of a partition of the interval \([a,b]\).

Front: {{c1::Formula for the lower Darboux sum}}

Front: What does it mean, intuitively, that \(f'\) has the intermediate value property?  
Back: It means \(f'\) cannot “jump” over values: whenever the derivative takes two values at nearby points, it must take every intermediate value somewhere in between.  



Front: Give an example of a differentiable function whose derivative is not continuous at 0.  
Back: \(f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr), & x\ne 0,\\[4pt] 0, & x=0,\end{cases}\) is differentiable everywhere, but \(f'\) is not continuous at \(0\).  



Front: What is the derivative of \(f(x) = x^2 \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\) for \(x\ne 0\) and at \(x=0\)?  
Back: For \(x\ne 0\), \(f'(x) = -\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr) + 2x \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\); for \(x=0\), \(f'(0)=0\).  



Front: For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,4]\) and partition \(Q=\{-1,0,2,4\}\), what is the upper Darboux sum \(U(f,Q)\)?  
Back: On \([-1,0]\) the maximum of \(x^2\) is 1; on \([0,2]\) it is 4; on \([2,4]\) it is 16. Thus \(U(f,Q)=1\cdot 1 + 4\cdot 2 + 16\cdot 2 = 41\).  



Front: For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,4]\) and partition \(Q=\{-1,0,2,4\}\), what is the lower Darboux sum \(L(f,Q)\)?  
Back: On \([-1,0]\) the minimum is 0; on \([0,2]\) it is 0; on \([2,4]\) it is 4. Thus \(L(f,Q)=0\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 2 + 4\cdot 2 = 8\).  



Front: For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,4]\) and coarse partition \(P=\{-1,4\}\), what are the upper and lower Darboux sums?  
Back: On \([-1,4]\) the maximum of \(x^2\) is 16 and the minimum is 0, with width \(5\). So \(U(f,P)=16\cdot5=80\) and \(L(f,P)=0\cdot5=0\).  



Front: Is the derivative of a differentiable function always continuous?  
Back: No. A function can be differentiable everywhere while its derivative has discontinuities.  



Front: What kinds of discontinuities can a derivative have?  
Back: A derivative cannot have jump, removable, or infinite (vertical asymptote) discontinuities; any discontinuity of a derivative must be an essential discontinuity.  



Front: Why can’t a derivative have a jump discontinuity?  
Back: Because by Theorem 29.8 the derivative has the intermediate value property, so it must take all values between nearby derivative values and therefore cannot “jump” over a gap.  



Front: In the informal picture of integration, how are upper and lower sums used to approximate the area under \(f\) on \([a,b]\)?  
Back: The interval \([a,b]\) is partitioned, heights are chosen using suprema (upper sums) or infima (lower sums) on each subinterval, and rectangle areas are summed; refining the partition makes these sums approach the true area.  



Front: Conceptually, what do the numbers \(M_k\) and \(m_k\) represent in the definitions of \(U(f,P)\) and \(L(f,P)\)?  
Back: \(M_k\) is the largest value of \(f\) on the subinterval \([t_{k-1},t_k]\), giving the height of the \(k\)-th upper rectangle; \(m_k\) is the smallest value of \(f\) there, giving the height of the \(k\)-th lower rectangle.  



Front: True or false: If \(f'\) has the intermediate value property on \((a,b)\), then \(f'\) must be continuous on \((a,b)\).  
Back: False. The example \(f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)\) for \(x\ne 0\), \(f(0)=0\), has a derivative with the intermediate value property but a discontinuity at 0; Darboux’s theorem does not imply continuity.  



Front: True or false: A derivative can have a jump discontinuity.  
Back: False. Because derivatives satisfy the intermediate value property, they cannot jump over values; any discontinuity must be essential.  



Front: Suppose \(f\) is differentiable on \((a,b)\) and \(f'(x_1)=-1\), \(f'(x_2)=3\) with \(a<x_1<x_2<b\). What does Theorem 29.8 guarantee?  
Back: It guarantees there exists \(x_0\in(x_1,x_2)\) with \(f'(x_0)=0\), since 0 lies between \(-1\) and \(3\).  



Front: For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,4]\), compare the upper and lower sums for partitions \(P=\{-1,4\}\) and \(Q=\{-1,0,2,4\}\). Which are larger?  
Back: The finer partition \(Q\) gives \(U(f,Q)=41 < U(f,P)=80\) and \(L(f,Q)=8 > L(f,P)=0\); refining partitions lowers upper sums and raises lower sums for this example.  



Front: Explain how the example \(f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)\) (with \(f(0)=0\)) shows that differentiability does not imply continuity of the derivative.  
Back: \(f\) is differentiable everywhere, but \(f'(x)\) oscillates between \(-1\) and \(1\) near \(0\) and does not converge to \(f'(0)=0\); thus \(f'\) is discontinuous at \(0\) even though \(f\) is differentiable there.


Front: Definition of the upper Darboux integral of a bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\)  
Back: The upper Darboux integral of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) is \(U(f) = \inf\{U(f,P) : P \text{ is a partition of } [a,b]\}.\)  



Front: Definition of the lower Darboux integral of a bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\)  
Back: The lower Darboux integral of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) is \(L(f) = \sup\{L(f,P) : P \text{ is a partition of } [a,b]\}.\)  



Front: {{c1::Upper Darboux integral formula}}  
Back: {{c1::For bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\), \(U(f) = \inf\{U(f,P) : P \text{ a partition of } [a,b]\}.\}}  



Front: {{c1::Lower Darboux integral formula}}  
Back: {{c1::For bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\), \(L(f) = \sup\{L(f,P) : P \text{ a partition of } [a,b]\}.\}}  



Front: Definition of Darboux integrability  
Back: A bounded function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is (Darboux) integrable on \([a,b]\) if \(L(f) = U(f)\). In this case we write \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).  



Front: {{c1::Condition for a bounded function \(f\) to be integrable on \([a,b]\)}}  
Back: {{c1::\(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) if and only if \(L(f) = U(f)\).}}  



Front: Lemma 32.2 (relationship between partitions)  
Back: If \(P \subseteq Q\) are partitions of \([a,b]\) and \(f\) is bounded on \([a,b]\), then \(L(f,P) \le L(f,Q) \le U(f,Q) \le U(f,P).\)  



Front: {{c1::Inequality chain relating lower and upper sums for nested partitions}}  
Back: {{c1::If \(P \subseteq Q\), then \(L(f,P) \le L(f,Q) \le U(f,Q) \le U(f,P)\).}}  



Front: Lemma 32.3 (lower sums are always \(\le\) upper sums)  
Back: For any partitions \(P_1,P_2\) of \([a,b]\) and bounded \(f\), we have \(L(f,P_1) \le U(f,P_2)\).  



Front: {{c1::Statement of Lemma 32.3 in terms of arbitrary partitions}}  
Back: {{c1::For any partitions \(P_1,P_2\) of \([a,b]\) and bounded \(f\), \(L(f,P_1) \le U(f,P_2)\).}}  



Front: State Theorem 32.4 relating upper and lower integrals  
Back: If \(f\) is bounded on \([a,b]\), then the lower integral is always less than or equal to the upper integral: \(L(f) \le U(f)\).  



Front: {{c1::Inequality between lower and upper Darboux integrals for a bounded function}}  
Back: {{c1::For bounded \(f\) on \([a,b]\), \(L(f) \le U(f)\).}}  



Front: Conceptual meaning of the inequality \(L(f) \le U(f)\)  
Back: Lower sums approximate the area from below and upper sums from above. Taking the supremum of all lower sums and the infimum of all upper sums cannot reverse the inequality, so the best lower approximation never exceeds the best upper approximation.  



Front: For a constant function \(f(x)=c\) on \([a,b]\), what are the upper and lower Darboux sums \(U(f,P)\) and \(L(f,P)\) for any partition \(P\)?  
Back: On each subinterval, \(\sup f = \inf f = c\), so \(U(f,P) = L(f,P) = c(b-a)\) for every partition \(P\).  



Front: For the constant function \(f(x)=c\) on \([a,b]\), what are \(U(f)\) and \(L(f)\)? Is \(f\) integrable?  
Back: We have \(U(f)=L(f)=c(b-a)\), so \(f\) is integrable and \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = c(b-a)\).  



Front: Consider \(g(x)=\begin{cases}c, & x\in\mathbb{Q},\\\\ 0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q},\end{cases}\) on \([a,b]\). What are \(U(g,P)\) and \(L(g,P)\) for any partition \(P\)?  
Back: On every subinterval, \(\sup g = c\) and \(\inf g = 0\). Thus for any partition \(P\), \(U(g,P) = c(b-a)\) and \(L(g,P) = 0\).  



Front: For the function \(g(x)=c\) on rationals and \(0\) on irrationals in \([a,b]\), compute \(U(g)\) and \(L(g)\). Is \(g\) integrable?  
Back: We have \(U(g) = c(b-a)\) and \(L(g) = 0\). If \(c \ne 0\), then \(U(g) \ne L(g)\), so \(g\) is not integrable. If \(c=0\), then \(g\equiv 0\) and is integrable.  



Front: {{c1::Key difference in integrability behavior between the constant function \(f(x)=c\) and the rational/irrational function \(g(x)\)}}  
Back: {{c1::For \(f(x)=c\), all upper and lower sums coincide; for \(g(x)\), all upper sums equal \(c(b-a)\) and all lower sums equal 0, so \(L(g)\ne U(g)\) when \(c\ne0\).}}  



Front: Conceptual question: How does refining a partition affect upper and lower sums according to Lemma 32.2?  
Back: Refining a partition (adding more points) cannot decrease lower sums or increase upper sums: lower sums go up or stay the same, and upper sums go down or stay the same.  



Front: Conceptual question: Strategy used in the proof of Theorem 32.4  
Back: Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose a partition \(P_1\) so that \(U(f,P_1) < U(f)+\varepsilon\) and a partition \(P_2\) so that \(L(f,P_2) > L(f)-\varepsilon\); then use Lemma 32.3 to compare \(L(f,P_2)\) and \(U(f,P_1)\) and let \(\varepsilon \to 0\) to conclude \(L(f)\le U(f)\).  



Front: Challenge: True or false? If there exists a partition \(P\) with \(U(f,P) = L(f,P)\), then \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).  
Back: True. If some partition has equal upper and lower sums, then all refinements have equal sums, so \(\sup L(f,P) = \inf U(f,P)\) and hence \(L(f)=U(f)\).  



Front: Challenge: Explain why the function \(g(x)=c\) on rationals and \(0\) on irrationals fails to be integrable when \(c\ne0\).  
Back: Because for every partition, upper sums are \(c(b-a)\) and lower sums are 0, so the lower integral is 0 and the upper integral is \(c(b-a)\); since they differ, the integrability condition \(L(g)=U(g)\) fails.
Front: State Theorem 33.1 about monotone functions and integrability on \([a,b]\).
Back: Theorem 33.1: Every monotonic function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).



Front: {{c1::Main content of Theorem 33.1}}
Back: {{c1::Every monotonic function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).}}



Front: Conceptual question: Why does monotonicity help guarantee integrability?
Back: A monotone function on \([a,b]\) can only have jump discontinuities, and at most countably many of them. Such functions still admit upper and lower sums that can be made arbitrarily close, so by Theorem 33.1 every monotone function on \([a,b]\) is integrable.



Front: State Theorem 33.2 about continuity and integrability.
Back: Theorem 33.2: Every continuous function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).



Front: {{c1::Relationship between continuity and integrability from Theorem 33.2}}
Back: {{c1::If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).}}



Front: True or false: Every integrable function on \([a,b]\) is continuous on \([a,b]\).
Back: False. Theorems 33.1 and 33.2 say that every monotone or continuous function is integrable, but there are integrable functions with jump discontinuities, so integrability does not imply continuity.



Front: State Theorem 33.3(i) about scalar multiples of integrable functions.
Back: Theorem 33.3(i): If \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(c\in\mathbb{R}\), then \(cf\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(\displaystyle \int_a^b cf = c \int_a^b f.\)



Front: State Theorem 33.3(ii) about sums of integrable functions.
Back: Theorem 33.3(ii): If \(f\) and \(g\) are integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(f+g\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(\displaystyle \int_a^b (f+g) = \int_a^b f + \int_a^b g.\)



Front: {{c1::Linearity of the integral from Theorem 33.3}}
Back: {{c1::If \(f,g\) are integrable and \(c\in\mathbb{R}\), then \(cf\) and \(f+g\) are integrable and \(\int_a^b (cf + g) = c\int_a^b f + \int_a^b g.\)}}



Front: State Theorem 33.4(i) (comparison theorem for integrals).
Back: Theorem 33.4(i): If \(f\) and \(g\) are integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(f(x) \le g(x)\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\), then \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g.\)



Front: {{c1::Inequality relating \(f\) and \(g\) in Theorem 33.4(i)}}
Back: {{c1::If \(f\le g\) pointwise on \([a,b]\) and both are integrable, then \(\int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g\).}}



Front: State Theorem 33.4(ii) about nonnegative continuous functions with zero integral.
Back: Theorem 33.4(ii): If \(g\) is a continuous nonnegative function on \([a,b]\) and \(\displaystyle \int_a^b g = 0\), then \(g\) is identically zero on \([a,b]\).



Front: Conceptual meaning of Theorem 33.4(ii).
Back: If a continuous function \(g\ge 0\) on \([a,b]\) has integral zero, then it must satisfy \(g(x)=0\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\); a continuous nonnegative graph can have zero “area” only if it lies on the \(x\)-axis.



Front: State Theorem 33.5.
Back: Theorem 33.5: If \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(\displaystyle \left|\int_a^b f\right| \le \int_a^b |f|.\)



Front: {{c1::First part of Theorem 33.5}}
Back: {{c1::If \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).}}



Front: {{c1::Inequality in Theorem 33.5 relating \(f\) and \(|f|\)}}
Back: {{c1::For integrable \(f\), \(\left|\int_a^b f\right| \le \int_a^b |f|.\)}}



Front: What is the contrapositive of the first part of Theorem 33.5?
Back: Theorem 33.5 says: if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\). Its contrapositive is: if \(|f|\) is not integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is not integrable on \([a,b]\). This contrapositive is logically equivalent to Theorem 33.5.



Front: True or false: If \(|f|\) is not integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is not integrable on \([a,b]\).
Back: True. This is exactly the contrapositive of the first part of Theorem 33.5, which states: if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).



Front: What is the converse of the first part of Theorem 33.5?
Back: The first part of Theorem 33.5 says: if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\). Its converse is: if \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).



Front: True or false: The converse of the first part of Theorem 33.5 is true.
Back: False. Theorem 33.5 states that if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(\left|\int_a^b f\right| \le \int_a^b |f|\). The converse "if \(|f|\) is integrable, then \(f\) is integrable" need not hold; one can construct bounded functions \(f\) for which \(|f|\) is integrable but \(f\) is not Riemann integrable.



Front: Example-style question: Which of the following corresponds to the converse of the statement "If \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\)"?
Back: The converse is: "If \(|f|\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\)." The original is Theorem 33.5; its contrapositive is "If \(|f|\) is not integrable, then \(f\) is not integrable."



Front: Challenge: Explain how Theorem 33.5 guides your reasoning about integrability when given information about \(f\) versus \(|f|\).
Back: Theorem 33.5 tells us: (1) If \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then we automatically know \(|f|\) is integrable and \(\left|\int f\right| \le \int |f|\). (2) By contrapositive, if \(|f|\) is not integrable, then \(f\) cannot be integrable. (3) However, the converse is false: even if \(|f|\) is integrable, we cannot conclude from Theorem 33.5 alone that \(f\) is integrable.
