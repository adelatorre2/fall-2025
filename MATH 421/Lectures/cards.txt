Why does Bolzano–Weierstrass follow from Theorem 11.4 + Theorem 10.2?

**Back:**

Thus, bounded sequence ⇒ monotonic subsequence ⇒ convergent subsequence.

**Back:**  
It guarantees that **bounded sequences always have some form of convergence** (via subsequences), even if the full sequence does not converge.  
It underlies compactness in \(\mathbb{R}\) and is foundational in analysis.

**Front:**  
What does a bounded sequence mean?

**Back:**  
A sequence \((s_n)\) is bounded if there is some number \(M > 0\) such that  
\[
|s_n| \le M \quad \text{for all } n.
\]

**Back:**  
Because bounded sequences cannot “escape to infinity,” and by the infinite pigeonhole principle + monotonic subsequence theorem, you can always extract a subsequence that converges.

# ✅ **ANKI FLASHCARDS — Theorem 11.7, limsup, liminf, subsequential limits**

---

**Theorem 11.7 (statement)**  
	For any sequence \((s_n)\), there exists a monotonic subsequence whose limit is \(\limsup s_n\), and there exists a monotonic subsequence whose limit is \(\liminf s_n\).

---

**Definition of limsup \(s_n\)**  
	\(\limsup s_n\) is the limit of the sequence \(\sup\{s_n : n > N\}\) as \(N \to \infty\).

---

**Definition of liminf \(s_n\)**  
	\(\liminf s_n\) is the limit of the sequence \(\inf\{s_n : n > N\}\) as \(N \to \infty\).

---

**Intuition: limsup**  
	\(\limsup s_n\) is the “eventual supremum” — the largest value that subsequences can accumulate near infinitely often.

---

**Intuition: liminf**  
	\(\liminf s_n\) is the “eventual infimum” — the smallest value that subsequences can accumulate near infinitely often.

---

**Connection to subsequential limits**  
	The limsup is the **largest subsequential limit**, and the liminf is the **smallest subsequential limit**.

---

**All subsequential limits lie between liminf and limsup**  
	For every subsequential limit \(t\), we have \(\liminf s_n \le t \le \limsup s_n\).

---

**Characterization of limsup in terms of subsequential limits**  
	\(\limsup s_n = \sup\{t : t \text{ is a subsequential limit of } s_n\}\).

---

**Characterization of liminf in terms of subsequential limits**  
	\(\liminf s_n = \inf\{t : t \text{ is a subsequential limit of } s_n\}\).

---

**Why monotonic subsequences exist (Theorem 11.4)**  
	Every sequence has a monotonic subsequence, found by tracking dominant or non-dominant indices.

---

**Why monotonic subsequences help with limsup/liminf**  
	Monotonic sequences have limits (finite or infinite), so finding monotonic subsequences allows you to produce actual limits equal to limsup or liminf.

---

**If a sequence is unbounded above**  
	Then some monotonic subsequence diverges to \(+\infty\), which equals \(\limsup s_n\).

---

**If a sequence is unbounded below**  
	Then some monotonic subsequence diverges to \(-\infty\), which equals \(\liminf s_n\).

---

**Key step in the limsup proof (bounded-above case)**  
	Let \(t = \limsup s_n\). For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), eventually all tail suprema satisfy \(\sup\{s_n : n > N\} < t + \varepsilon\).

---

**Why the set \(\{n : t - \varepsilon < s_n < t + \varepsilon\}\) must be infinite**  
	If it were finite, tails would be bounded by \(t - \varepsilon\), contradicting the definition of limsup.

---

**From the infinite set condition to a subsequence**  
	Because infinitely many terms lie in any neighborhood around \(t\), we can extract an increasing subsequence converging to \(t = \limsup s_n\).

---

**Parallel fact for liminf**  
	Infinitely many terms lie within any neighborhood of \(\liminf s_n\), letting us extract a subsequence decreasing to \(\liminf s_n\).

---

**Interpretation of Theorem 11.7**  
	The limsup and liminf are always realized by actual subsequences, not just abstract bounds.

---

**Connection to Bolzano–Weierstrass**  
	If the sequence is bounded, then by Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a convergent subsequence; Theorem 11.7 identifies the **extreme** limits of such subsequences.

# **1. Start With Subsequence Definition (11.1)**

### Core idea  
A *subsequence* is made by selecting terms of the original sequence but keeping their order:
\[
t_k = s_{n_k},\quad n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \cdots
\]

### Why it matters  
Subsequences allow us to “zoom in” on particular long-term behaviors inside a sequence.  
Even if the full sequence doesn't converge, some subsequences might.

---

# **2. Every Sequence Has a Monotone Subsequence (11.4)**

### Why this is huge:
Monotone sequences always converge (possibly to \(\pm\infty\)).  
So:

**Every sequence has *at least one* subsequence that converges.**

This guarantees the set of subsequential limits is **never empty**.

This is the foundation for limsup/liminf.

---

# **3. Learn Definition of Subsequential Limits (11.6)**

A subsequential limit is:
> “Any limit of any subsequence.”

A sequence may have  
• one subsequential limit → implies convergence  
• several → oscillation  
• infinitely many → dense behavior (e.g., rational enumeration)  
• \(\pm\infty\) → divergence in that direction

---

# **4. Theorem 11.7 – Existence of Monotone Subsequences Reaching limsup/liminf**

This theorem connects subsequences to limsup/liminf:

- There exists a subsequence whose limit is \(\limsup s_n\).  
- There exists a subsequence whose limit is \(\liminf s_n\).

This tells us:

> **limsup = largest subsequential limit**  
> **liminf = smallest subsequential limit**

---

# **5. Theorem 11.8 – The Core Framework**

Let \(S\) be the set of subsequential limits.

1. **Nonempty** (from monotone subsequences).  
2.  
\[
\sup S = \limsup s_n,\quad \inf S = \liminf s_n.
\]
3.  
\[
\lim s_n \text{ exists } \iff S \text{ has exactly one element.}
\]

This theorem is the *bridge* that lets you compute limsup/liminf by finding subsequences.
